Notebook 04: Machine learning on the climbers¶

Project: Hacker Statistics, chances of winning a bet
Notebooks 02 and 03 used exact math and statistics. This notebook asks what machine learning adds. Normally an ML model is judged against other models, with no way to know how good is good enough. Here we have an answer key: from notebook 02, the exact chance of winning from any position. So we can say precisely how close each model gets to the best possible score, and whether a fancier model has anything left to find.

Techniques covered, all with scikit-learn: logistic regression, a small neural network, gradient boosting, train and test splits, calibration, learning curves, k-means clustering and isolation-forest anomaly detection.

How each section is laid out: what we are looking for, the code, its result, how to read it, what it means, and what's next and why.

1. Setup¶

What we are looking for¶

Imports and the shared game settings, plus the exact value map from notebook 02. We will use it as the answer key.

How to read it¶

The last line confirms the value map loaded. It has one row for each roll from 0 to 100 and one column for each possible step.

What it means¶

V[30, 40] is the true chance of winning for a climber standing on step 40 after 30 rolls. It is the best any prediction can possibly do for that climber, which is what makes the scoring honest.

What's next and why¶

Next we build the machine learning problem: a table of climbers, what we know about each at roll 30, and whether they eventually won.

2. The prediction problem¶

What we are looking for¶

A classic supervised-learning setup. The question: halfway through the first third of the game, at roll 30, can we predict who will win? The data: 20,000 simulated climbers. For each, the input is where they stand at roll 30 and the label is win or lose after roll 100. We split them 70% for training and 30% for testing, so that every score is measured on climbers the model never saw.

In [2]:
K = 30                                                    # we predict at roll 30
paths = simulate(walks=20_000, rolls=ROLLS, clumsy=CLUMSY, seed=2024)
y = (paths[:, -1] >= TARGET).astype(int)                  # 1 = won
x = paths[:, K].reshape(-1, 1).astype(float)              # the one thing we know: current step

train, test = train_test_split(np.arange(len(y)), test_size=0.3, random_state=0, stratify=y)
print(f"Climbers: {len(y):,}   training: {len(train):,}   testing: {len(test):,}")
print(f"Share who win (training): {y[train].mean():.2%}   (testing): {y[test].mean():.2%}")
print(f"Steps at roll {K}: lowest {int(x.min())}, median {int(np.median(x))}, highest {int(x.max())}")

How to read it¶

The split is stratified, so the share of winners is nearly identical in training and testing (78.1% and 78.1%). The last line shows how much climbers differ at roll 30, from step 0 up to the highest finisher.

What it means¶

About 78 climbers in 100 win no matter what. So a lazy predictor that says "everyone wins" is right 78% of the time. That is the number to beat. A model's score only means something compared with this baseline.

What's next and why¶

Before training anything, we compute the best possible scores from the answer key, so we know the finish line.

3. The answer key: the best possible predictor¶

What we are looking for¶

The score a perfect predictor would get on the test climbers. For a climber on step s at roll 30 the perfect prediction is exactly V[30, s]. No model, however clever, can beat it on average, because it knows the true rules of the game. We score three things: accuracy (how often the yes/no call is right), log loss (how well the probabilities are calibrated, lower is better) and AUC (how well the predictions rank winners above losers, 0.5 is a coin flip, 1.0 is perfect).

In [3]:
def scores(y_true, prob):
    return accuracy_score(y_true, prob > 0.5), log_loss(y_true, prob), roc_auc_score(y_true, prob)

results = {}
results["Always say 'win' (baseline)"] = scores(y[test], np.full(len(test), y[train].mean()))
results["Exact answer (the best possible)"] = scores(y[test], V[K, paths[test, K]])

def show(results):
    print(f"{'Predictor':<38}{'Accuracy':>10}{'Log loss':>10}{'AUC':>8}")
    for name, (a, l, u) in results.items():
        print(f"{name:<38}{a:>10.2%}{l:>10.4f}{u:>8.3f}")
show(results)

Always say 'win' (baseline)               78.12%    0.5254   0.500
Exact answer (the best possible)          78.35%    0.4791   0.706

How to read it¶

Each row is a predictor and each column a score. For accuracy higher is better, for AUC higher is better, and for log loss lower is better. The baseline is the lazy "everyone wins".

What it means¶

Even the perfect predictor is only 78.4% accurate, barely above the lazy baseline's 78.1%. At roll 30 the game is still too undecided for anyone to say much: the best possible AUC is just 0.706. The perfect predictor's advantage shows mainly in log loss (0.479 against 0.525 for the baseline), meaning its probabilities are better even where its yes/no calls are not.

That is the real lesson in this notebook. When outcomes are mostly luck, the ceiling is low and no model can pass it.

What's next and why¶

Now we train real models. The question is how close each gets to this ceiling.

4. Three models on one feature¶

What we are looking for¶

How close logistic regression, a small neural network and gradient boosting get to the answer key, using only the current step.

  • Logistic regression: fits one smooth S-shaped curve. The simplest classifier there is.
  • Neural network: one hidden layer of 8 units with tanh activation. Much more flexible, so it could in principle learn any shape.
  • Gradient boosting: builds hundreds of small decision trees, each correcting the last. The workhorse of tabular machine learning.

All three are trained on the 14,000 training climbers only and scored on the 6,000 test climbers.

How to read it¶

The table adds three rows. The chart plots each model's predicted chance of winning for every possible step at roll 30; the thick grey line is the exact answer. A model whose line sits on the grey one has learned the true rule.

What it means¶

All three models score the same as the answer key to three decimal places: accuracy near 78.5%, log loss near 0.479 and AUC 0.706. (On this particular test set the models' log loss is even a hair better than the answer key's, 0.4788 against 0.4791. That is luck of the draw in 6,000 climbers, not a model beating the truth.) The chart shows where they differ. Up to about step 35, where almost all climbers are, the logistic regression and the neural network track the exact curve closely, and gradient boosting follows it too but with jagged wobbles. Beyond that the exact curve flattens at about 93%, because even a climber far ahead can still be clumsy. The logistic regression keeps climbing toward 100% and the neural network toward 98%, so both are overconfident for the few climbers who are far ahead. Very few test climbers are out there (the highest at roll 30 is step 61), so it barely moves the scores, but it is a real weakness of a smooth S-curve fitted to a curve that levels off.

So the neural network, with its extra flexibility, found nothing important that the simple S-curve missed, and gradient boosting found nothing either. They all had the same single clue, and a clever model cannot extract more than the clue contains.

What's next and why¶

If one feature is the limit, perhaps more features would help. We give the models the climber's history and see.

5. Does more information help? (the Markov property)¶

What we are looking for¶

Whether adding each climber's history improves the prediction. We add four extra features: the step at roll 10, the step at roll 20, the highest step reached so far and the number of times the climber has been on step 0. The mathematics of the game says history should not matter. Where you can go next depends only on where you stand now, a property called the Markov property. If the models agree, we have confirmed it from the data.

In [5]:
history = np.column_stack([paths[:, K],                       # step now
                           paths[:, 10], paths[:, 20],         # where they were earlier
                           paths[:, :K + 1].max(axis=1),       # best step so far
                           (paths[:, 1:K + 1] == 0).sum(axis=1)]).astype(float)   # times on step 0

more = {
    "Logistic regression + history": make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression()),
    "Gradient boosting + history":   HistGradientBoostingClassifier(random_state=0),
}
compare = {k: results[k] for k in ["Exact answer (the best possible)", "Logistic regression", "Gradient boosting"]}
for name, model in more.items():
    model.fit(history[train], y[train])
    compare[name] = scores(y[test], model.predict_proba(history[test])[:, 1])
show(compare)

How to read it¶

The top three rows repeat the one-feature results. The bottom two rows have the same models but five features instead of one.

What it means¶

History does not help. Logistic regression with history scores the same as without (log loss 0.4788 either way) and gradient boosting is slightly worse with the extra features (0.482 against 0.480). The extra columns add noise and give a flexible model more chances to fit coincidences in the training data (overfitting).

This is the Markov property confirmed from data. The lesson applies well beyond this game: extra features can only help if they carry information the existing ones lack. More columns are not automatically better.

What's next and why¶

We saw that at roll 30 the ceiling is low. Next we ask a natural follow-up: how does the ceiling change as the game goes on?

6. When does the answer become knowable?¶

What we are looking for¶

How the best-possible AUC grows with the roll number. At roll 0 nothing is known. At roll 100 the outcome is certain. In between we want to see how early a good prediction becomes possible. We use the exact value map as the predictor at each roll, scored on all 20,000 climbers.

The red line is the best any predictor could do at each point in the game. The bottom dotted line is a coin flip. The higher the line, the more the position tells you about the final result.

What it means¶

The game becomes predictable slowly. After 10 rolls the best possible AUC is only 0.60, after 30 it is 0.71 and after 50 it is 0.81. It reaches 0.89 at roll 70 and 0.96 at roll 90, and is 0.995 at roll 99 (not exactly 1, because the last roll can still change the result). Most of the uncertainty is resolved in the second half.

The practical meaning: any early-warning model for this game is limited by chance, not by how clever the model is. In real problems the same thing happens, and it helps to know the ceiling before spending effort on a more complicated model.

What's next and why¶

We have used 14,000 climbers to train. Do the models actually need that many? A learning curve answers it.

What's next and why¶

So far every model was supervised: it was told who won. Next we try unsupervised learning, where the model finds structure by itself, without being given the answer.

8. Finding types of climbers (k-means clustering)¶

What we are looking for¶

Groups of similar climbers, found without using the win/loss label at all. Each climber is described by their step at rolls 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50, the first half of the game. k-means places a chosen number of group centres and assigns each climber to the nearest. We afterwards look at how often each group wins, which is a check the clustering never saw.

First the harder question, how many groups? We compare k = 2 to 7 with the silhouette score, which measures how cleanly separated the groups are (higher is better, 1 is perfect).

In [8]:
early = paths[:, [10, 20, 30, 40, 50]].astype(float)

print(f"{'Groups (k)':<12}{'Silhouette':>12}{'Total spread (inertia)':>26}")
for k in range(2, 8):
    km = KMeans(k, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit(early)
    sil = silhouette_score(early, km.labels_, sample_size=5000, random_state=0)

How to read it¶

The first table compares group counts. The silhouette score is highest at k = 2 (0.41) and falls steadily after that; the total spread keeps falling no matter what, so it cannot choose k by itself. The second table describes four groups. The chart draws each group's average path through the first 50 rolls, with the group's eventual win rate in the legend.

What it means¶

The clustering never saw who won, yet the groups separate winners from losers cleanly: fast climbers win 95% of the time and steady climbers 91%, while slow climbers win 78% and the stuck group only 46%. The groups were found from shape alone and the win rates confirm they mean something.

The choice of four is a judgement call and worth saying plainly. The silhouette score prefers two groups, and four is not "the true number". Real climbers vary smoothly, not in clumps, so any k is a convenient way of slicing a continuum. Four tells a more useful story than two, but it is a choice, not a discovery.

What's next and why¶

Clustering found the typical types. The last technique looks for the opposite: climbers that don't fit any type.

9. Spotting unusual climbers (isolation forest)¶

What we are looking for¶

Climbers whose first 50 rolls look unlike the rest. Isolation forest works by repeatedly splitting the data at random. A climber who is unusual gets isolated after only a few splits; an ordinary one takes many. We ask it to flag the most unusual 1% (200 of 20,000) and then see what kinds of climbers they are and how they end up.

The table splits the flagged climbers into the fast and the slow, and shows how each group finishes. The chart draws 150 ordinary climbers in grey and four flagged ones in colour; the dotted line is where the model stopped looking.

What it means¶

"Unusual" turns out to mean two opposite things. Of the 200 flagged climbers, 152 (76%) are unusually fast (and win 95% of the time) and 22% are unusually slow (and win only about one in five). The rest sit in between. Together they win at about the same rate as everyone else, 79% against 78%, so being unusual says nothing about winning by itself. The average hides two groups that cancel out.

The lesson for anomaly detection: it tells you which cases are different, not whether the difference is good or bad. A person still has to look at the flagged cases and decide what they mean.

Takeaways

  1. Know the ceiling. With an answer key, we could tell when a model had finished its job.
  2. A more complicated model does not beat a simple one when the information is the limit.
  3. Extra features only help when they carry new information.
  4. Unsupervised methods find structure, but a human decides which structure matters.
